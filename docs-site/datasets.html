How validation works

The OceanVal app takes you from a folder of model output to a validation report, with no Python. Run oceanval in a terminal and choose Validate simulations:

  1. Give the directory the simulation’s output is in. OceanVal reads the years it covers from the files’ time axes.
  2. Choose the observations to validate against, first gridded then point datasets. OceanVal finds the simulation variable for each observed variable and ticks the datasets it can use; each needs the simulation years to validate (From and To). Add observations of your own if you have them.
  3. Check the units, and say you are happy with the matchups OceanVal found.
  4. Choose where the matchups are saved and what to do with any that exist already, then set the report options, the number of cores and what counts as a missing value.
  5. Watch the run: a grid shows each variable and data source as it is matched up, and an interim report grows as each is finished.

The result is an HTML report you can share, optionally exported to PDF and Word. The full steps are in the OceanVal app; to validate from a script instead, see How to use in Python. Downloaded observations can be kept for later runs in a data store.

Built-in observational datasets

OceanVal has built-in access to widely used observational datasets, gridded and point, covering temperature, salinity, oxygen (with apparent oxygen utilization and saturation), nutrients, chlorophyll, the carbonate system, surface pCO2 and the air-sea CO2 flux, dissolved and particulate organic carbon, the carbon biomass of plankton groups (diatoms, picophytoplankton, bacteria, micro- and mesozooplankton) and light attenuation, globally, on the Northwest European Shelf and in European seas. The tables below are the ones the app offers: the gridded datasets, then the point datasets.

In the OceanVal app, the datasets are offered in the step where you choose the observations to validate against. OceanVal reads your simulation, works out which of its variables holds each observed variable, and ticks every dataset for your simulation’s domain that it finds a variable for. The domain is worked out for you: if more than 80% of the cells of your simulation lie inside the Northwest European Shelf (the “Full Domain” of OceanVal’s nwes regions file) it is the shelf, otherwise it is global. Check what it found, and untick anything you do not want.

The observations are downloaded for you when the matchup runs. World Ocean Atlas 2023, the North Sea Biogeochemical Climatology, Ocean Colour CCI, COBE-SST 2 and NOAA OISST v2.1 come from THREDDS servers, so only what is needed is downloaded; NOAA CoralTemp v3.1 comes from NOAA CoastWatch’s ERDDAP server, which cuts out your simulation’s area and years before sending them, a year at a time; GLODAPv2.2016b is downloaded from NCEI as one file per variable; MPI-SOM-FFN, SOCAT and COPEPOD are downloaded whole and made into a netCDF file OceanVal can read; the GLODAPv3 and Hansell et al. compilations are one large file each, downloaded once and read for the variables you match up; ICES is downloaded for the years and area of the matchup. The server for COBE-SST 2 and NOAA OISST v2.1 limits how many requests it answers (HTTP 429), and when it turns them away OceanVal downloads the whole file instead. To keep what is downloaded for later matchups, set a data store in the app’s Preferences. The model output and the observations are then put on a common grid and averaged by month and year, where that is appropriate.

World Ocean Atlas 2023 temperature and salinity

The World Ocean Atlas’s temperature and salinity are decadal climatologies, one for each of these periods:

  • 1955–1964
  • 1965–1974
  • 1975–1984
  • 1985–1994
  • 1995–2004
  • 2005–2014
  • 2015–2022

The years you match up have to fall within one period: choose the period your simulation covers, and set the years in the app to it, such as 1995 to 2004. Years that straddle two periods, such as 2000 to 2008, are refused, as is anything longer than a decade, so validate one period at a time. The nutrients, oxygen, apparent oxygen utilization and oxygen saturation use a single long-term climatology, and can be matched up with any years.

What point datasets are available?

Point datasets have no grid: each observation is a single sample at its own station, depth and date, so the spatial and temporal resolution is that of the samples. Point datasets are measurements at particular places, times and depths. The global ones are bottle samples from research cruises, GLODAPv3 and the Hansell et al. compilation of organic carbon. The European ones, covering European and neighbouring seas, are measurements at sea from the ICES Oceanographic database, downloaded for the years you match up, in the area given by the longitude and latitude limits you set in the app or, if you set none, the area your simulation covers. See how GLODAPv3 and Hansell et al. are treated and how ICES data is treated for what is kept.

How GLODAPv3 and Hansell et al. are treated

GLODAPv3 (Lange et al., 2026) is every bottle sample from 1181 cruises, 1972 to 2023, with the systematic differences between cruises adjusted away. It is one file, so it is downloaded whole, once, and each variable you match up is read from it. Only samples with a good WOCE flag (2) are kept.

Hansell et al. (Hansell et al., 2021) compiles dissolved organic carbon (1994–2021) and particulate organic carbon (1995–2018) from bottle samples on 223 cruises. Only samples with a good WOCE flag (2) and a value above zero are kept, and the pressure is converted to depth with TEOS-10.

A data store keeps the file once it is downloaded, so later matchups need not download it again.

How ICES data is treated

What is downloaded. For each variable, OceanVal asks ICES for an export of every profile in the area you match up (or your simulation’s own area) in the years you match up. Everything but temperature and salinity comes from the bottle and low resolution CTD data. Temperature and salinity take two exports, one of the high resolution CTD profiles and one of the bottle and low resolution CTD data, which are joined (see Profiles in both datasets below). An export holds every variable measured at its profiles, so large areas or long periods make for a large download.

Which values are used. Each variable is read from one ICES parameter code. ICES splits some analytes over several codes, by method, and only the code below is used: nitrate is nitrate alone, for example, not nitrate+nitrite.

Profiles in both datasets. Bottles are fired on CTD casts, so a cast can be in both the CTD and the bottle data, with the same values. For temperature and salinity, bottle values at a station the CTD data already has (the same date, and the same position to 0.01°) are left out, so each cast is counted once, and the run’s output says how many were. The bottle data adds the stations without a CTD profile, which are many in older years.

Quality flags. A value is kept only if its own quality flag and the flag of its depth are both 0 (good). Values flagged 1 (unknown quality), 4 (questionable) or 8 (bad) are left out. Recent years have many values flagged 1, so they can have few observations, or none.

Depth. The depth is ICES’s adopted depth (ADEPZZ01), in metres. Observations at 5 m or less make up the surface layer. Through the water column, an observation above the centre of the model’s top cell, as many bottle values at 0 m are, is compared with the top cell.

Time. Only the date of an observation is used, not the time of day. Times without a time zone are taken as UTC. A time written as hour 24 (1981-04-27T24) is 00:00 of the next day, as ISO 8601 has it. An observation dated only to the month or the year, or whose time cannot be read, is left out rather than given a day, and the run’s output says how many were.

Values. Values are used as ICES gives them, in the units in the table above. OceanVal does not screen them for range or remove duplicates, beyond the quality flags and the repeated stations above. ICES revises its data, so a later download can differ. With a data store set to always use what it has, the copy kept is used however old it is; the run says when it was downloaded.

Units

The Units columns give the units of the observations as each dataset’s files state them. Your model may use others, such as mmol/m³ for nutrients where the World Ocean Atlas uses µmol/kg. The app’s Units step lists the units of your model and of the observations side by side, suggests a conversion where it can tell how to convert one into the other, and asks you to confirm them before anything is matched up. Check the units, and for the World Ocean Atlas the period, every time.

Your own data

You can validate against observations of your own as well, point data in csv files or gridded data in netCDF files. Add them in the app’s Own data step for one matchup, or choose Add your own validation data for future use on its first page to keep them, so that later matchups offer them beside the built-in datasets. See the steps, and compatible data formats for how the files must be laid out.
